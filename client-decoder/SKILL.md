## Context: Why Indian Client Briefs Are Different

Indian residential clients â€” which form the majority of independent architectural practice in India â€” communicate in feelings, contradictions, and social signals. Understanding this context is essential to decoding correctly:

**The Joint Family Dynamic.** The person who calls you is rarely the only decision-maker. Typical residential project has 3-5 stakeholders with different and often conflicting requirements: the patriarch (status, first impression), the homemaker (function, flow, kitchen), the younger generation (aesthetic, technology), the elderly parent (accessibility, tradition, privacy), the children (just want their room). Each stakeholder gives different feedback and none of them have reconciled it internally before talking to you. A sixth stakeholder type â€” the NRI family member abroad â€” is especially common in metro cities: financially authoritative, aesthetically Westernised, and absent from all site meetings. Their preferences frequently conflict directly with the on-site family's.

**The Status Signal.** Indian residential architecture is deeply social. "Should look nice" almost always means "should signal status to neighbours and visitors." The street facade and entrance are performing for an audience. The interior is for the family. These are different briefs.

**Vastu Shastra.** Not a fringe preference â€” a genuine design constraint present in 60-70% of Indian residential projects across all income levels. Treat it as a hard structural requirement, not an afterthought. Surface it early, map the conflicts with site orientation and programme, and document it as a design constraint.

**The Indirect Budget.** Indian clients rarely state their budget directly. Budget anxiety is embedded in phrases like "keep it simple," "don't go overboard," "whatever it takes but be reasonable." Read these signals.

**The Reference Problem.** Clients show you photos from Pinterest, Instagram, or a neighbour's house. They cannot articulate which element they are pointing to â€” is it the massing, the material, the colour, the scale, the landscaping? Your job is to extract the specific transferable element from the reference, not copy the reference.

**Language Mixing.** Feedback arrives in Hinglish (Hindi + English), regional language mixed with English, or in WhatsApp voice notes. The emotional content is often in the regional language even when the structural feedback is in English. Treat both with equal importance.

## Process

### Step 1 â€” Identify the Input Type

Read the input and classify it as one of four types. This determines how to process it.

**Type A â€” Single Statement or Short Feedback**
One or a few sentences of client feedback. Either from a meeting, an email, or a message.
*Example: "I want it to look modern but also feel traditional. Grand entrance. Vastu is important."*

**Type B â€” Multi-Stakeholder Brief**
Multiple people's requirements listed, either by the architect from a meeting or pasted directly.
*Example: "Dad wants grand entrance. Mom wants vastu, separate puja room. Son wants open plan. Mother-in-law wants ground floor room."*

**Type C â€” WhatsApp Conversation**
A pasted thread of messages. May include timestamps, mixed language, contradictions across time, voice note references.
*Process rule: More recent messages override earlier ones. Treat the final 20% of the thread as the current brief. Flag contradictions with earlier messages.*

**Type D â€” Post-Design Review Feedback**
Client has seen a design and responded. Feedback is about what is wrong or missing, not what they want from scratch.
*Example: "The design is technically correct but the entrance feels like a corridor. And the living room gets too much afternoon sun."*

### Step 2 â€” Extract All Voices

For each piece of feedback, identify:
- **Who said it** (if known â€” patriarch, homemaker, young couple, elderly, etc.)
- **The surface statement** â€” exactly what they said
- **The underlying need** â€” what they actually want
- **The anxiety behind it** â€” what they are afraid of

If the input is a WhatsApp thread, extract client messages separately from architect messages. Ignore pleasantries and logistics ("ok see you Sunday"). Focus on anything about the design, the spaces, the feel, or the programme.

|-------------|--------------------------|

### Step 5 â€” Map Regional Aesthetic Vocabulary

When client uses words like "traditional," "like our hometown," or references a regional style, apply this guide:

**Kerala Traditional:**
Spatial references: Nalukettu (four-wing courtyard house), Ettu-kettu (eight-wing), Tharavadu
Key elements: Central courtyard (nadumuttam), sloped tiled roof (Mangalore tiles), wooden columns (teak), carved wooden screens (thevarappadam), open verandah (thinnai), narrow high windows, brick and laterite construction
Colours: Red oxide floors, dark teak wood, white lime-washed walls, red tile roofs
Contemporary translation: Sloped roof form, courtyard or light well, wooden screen elements, warm terracotta and wood palette

**Rajasthani / North-West Traditional:**
Spatial references: Haveli typology, multi-storey, enclosed courtyard
Key elements: Jaali screens (carved stone or brick perforations), Jharokha projecting bay windows, ornamental brackets (chajja supports), sandstone or limestone construction, step-well (baoli) references, painted facades in Shekhawati style
Colours: Sandstone gold, terracotta, ochre, deep blue accents, white lime
Contemporary translation: Jaali screen for privacy + light, projecting bay window element, sandstone or similar textured stone at facade, ornamental bracket reference at entrance canopy

**Tamil Nadu / South Traditional:**
Spatial references: Agraharam (linear street house), Chettinad mansion, Pillayar Kovil reference
Key elements: Thinnai (raised verandah at street edge), ornate doorway (traditional teak door with carved panels), central open courtyard, brick or stone construction, high ceilings (4-5m), octagonal columns in formal rooms
Colours: Deep red oxide floors, white lime, dark teak, terracotta
Contemporary translation: Thinnai element at entry, ornate teak door as focal piece, high ceilings, courtyard or light well, brick as expressed material

**Maharashtra / Konkan Traditional:**
Spatial references: Wada (fortified town house), Konkan house on slope
Key elements: Thick stone or brick walls, central courtyard (chowk), multiple generational levels, carved wooden balconies, deep protective overhangs, sloped roof in coastal areas
Colours: Dark basalt stone, white lime, red tile, carved dark wood
Contemporary translation: Deep overhanging roof, carved wood detail at balcony rail, stone base, courtyard as central organising space

**North India / Delhi / UP / Punjab:**
Spatial references: Kothi (bungalow) typology, Haveli-influenced
Key elements: Formal front lawn and gate, symmetrical facade, colonnaded verandah, double-height formal drawing room, separate service entrance, high boundary wall
Colours: Off-white or cream renders, stone base, occasionally brick, green lawn
Contemporary translation: Symmetrical composition, formal gate and driveway sequence, covered verandah element, grand foyer with double height

**Bengal / Odisha:**
Spatial references: Thakur Dalan (heritage mansion), traditional brick house
Key elements: Terracotta panels and motifs, brick construction, curved arched openings, projecting balconies with ornamental railings, high-ceilinged rooms, internal courtyard (uthon)
Colours: Brick red, cream, terracotta motif accents
Contemporary translation: Terracotta tile or panel as accent, arched or curved aperture reference, brick expression, internal light court

**Gujarat (Ahmedabad / Surat / Vadodara):**
Spatial references: Pol house (dense urban courtyard house), decorated timber facade, Haveli of merchant families
Key elements: Ornamental carved timber brackets at eaves and balconies, jali screens, turned wooden spindle balustrades, decorated facade tiles, dedicated Ganesh niche at entry (facing door, NE preferred), internal courtyard (chowk), thick load-bearing walls, merchant-house provision for ground-floor shop or display
Colours: White lime facade with warm stone base, natural teak wood, blue and green mosaic accent details in older tradition
Contemporary translation: Ornamental bracket reference at entry canopy, carved or laser-cut jali screen, teak balcony element with turned spindles, Ganesh niche at entry (non-negotiable for most Gujarati clients â€” ask if not mentioned), deep chajja/overhang for shading
Note: "Haveli jaisa touch" from a Gujarati client means this vocabulary â€” not Rajasthani sandstone or North Indian neo-classical columns. "Sheesham ka kaam" means solid rosewood joinery; no MDF or veneer substitutes. Trader-class clients (diamond, textile, pharma) frequently need a shop-front or display entry integrated into a residential brief â€” ask before assuming pure residential.

**Important â€” before applying any regional vocabulary:** Ask explicitly: "What is your family's native state and district?" and "Can you show one photo of a traditional home that feels right to you?" Do not infer regional vocabulary from a city of residence â€” a Tamil family in Mumbai for two generations, or an NRI couple building in Kerala but living in Canada, will not match the vocabulary of the city where the project is located.

### Step 6 â€” Surface Conflicts

After translating all feedback, explicitly list every contradiction. Do not smooth these over â€” surfacing them is the most valuable thing this process does.

**Common Indian residential conflicts to check for:**
- Vastu entry direction vs plot orientation vs street direction
- "Open kitchen" (young couple) vs "closed kitchen" (mother-in-law / smell / cooking style)
- "Modern exterior" vs "traditional interior feel" â€” are these actually in conflict or complementary?
- "Grand entrance" (status) vs "budget constraint" (keep it simple)
- "Privacy" vs "connectivity between spaces" (open plan)
- Elderly parent on ground floor vs parking/service functions on ground floor
- Joint family under one roof vs acoustic and visual privacy requirements

For each conflict state:
1. What the conflict is
2. Which stakeholder holds each position
3. Two or three design approaches that could resolve it
4. Which approach requires the least compromise

## Notes

- This skill decodes communication â€” it does not replace site analysis, structural assessment, or code compliance work.
- For Vastu conflicts, always present options to the client â€” never unilaterally override or silently ignore.
- If a client gives no information at all ("just make it nice"), respond with the 5 most important questions a new residential project requires before any design begins, rather than attempting a translation.
- If input is in Hindi or a regional language, decode and output in English but note the original phrase in brackets so the architect can reference it in client conversations.
- The WhatsApp message is the most important output for adoption. Architects who use the formal brief may not always â€” but if the WhatsApp message saves them 5 minutes of typing at 10pm, they will use this skill on every project.

**When client shares photo references:**
Note the photo as "[Visual reference shared]" and add to the "What You Still Need to Know" section: "Please describe what you liked about the photo you shared â€” is it the shape of the house from outside, the materials and colours, or how the inside feels?" Never guess what the photo contains.

**Developer / commercial clients:**
When the brief is from a developer (multi-unit complex, builder, commercial client) rather than a family, adjust tone. The WhatsApp message should be concise and business-like â€” not warm and conversational. Lead with risks and decisions to be made. The Stakeholder Map section is not needed for developer briefs.

**Post-review â€” confirm construction stage, not just construction status:**
When processing Type D (post-review) feedback, do not ask a yes/no. Ask for the specific site stage â€” the answer determines what is still changeable:
- Stage 1 â€” Not started: everything changeable
- Stage 2 â€” Excavation and foundation only: above-ground plan still fully changeable
- Stage 3 â€” Plinth and columns cast: structural grid locked; room sizes and positions largely fixed
- Stage 4 â€” First-floor slab poured: plumbing rough-in locked; kitchen and bathroom positions cannot move without breaking slab
- Stage 5 â€” Superstructure complete / finishing stage: only material, finish, and furniture changes are practical

Never recommend a spatial or structural change without knowing the stage. At Stages 4 and 5, frame all outputs as: "This can be changed but requires [specific work] â€” confirm cost and timeline with contractor before presenting to client."

**Vastu in multi-unit buildings:**
Full Vastu compliance for every apartment is physically impossible â€” units face multiple directions. When Vastu is requested for a multi-unit project, present three options: (1) Vastu-inspired layout principles applied where feasible, (2) Vastu consultant brought in to certify the overall development for marketing, (3) Vastu compliance guaranteed only for units in a specific block.
